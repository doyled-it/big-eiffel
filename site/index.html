NLDS Game 1 · American Family Field · October 2026

Would it have gone out?

Ty France hit a ball 105.3 mph at 49 degrees down the left-field line. It clipped a roof support cable near the top of its arc and was caught in left. Absent the cable, that ball reaches the 344 foot fence about one time in eight, and clears it for a home run about half as often.

11.8% chance of reaching 344 ft empirical, 95% interval 8 to 16%
319 ft expected carry about 25 ft short of the fence
~6% chance of a true home run over the 8 ft wall and fair; 4 to 10%

Exit velocity and launch angle are measured at the bat, before the ball nears the roof, so removing the cable changes none of the inputs. It only lets the ball finish its flight. The question is an ordinary one: how far does a ball hit this hard and this steep carry, and does it clear the fence fair? Three methods answer it, and they point the same way.

The evidence

What 280 comparable balls actually did

From 2015 to 2026, 280 balls were hit at the same speed and angle (104 to 106.5 mph, 48 to 50 degrees), none off a cable. Their mean carry was 319 feet, and 33, or 11.8 percent, reached 344. No model stands between the question and the answer here: these are real balls like France's. On the 2015 to 2025 regular-season subset the pipeline reproduces an established baseline exactly (250 balls, 31 cleared), which confirms the data and filter.

Why 49 degrees

A ball carries farthest near 30 degrees, not 49

At 49 degrees the ball climbs too steeply, trading distance for height. Hit this hard at 30 degrees it carries past 400 feet; by 49 it is down near 316. The physics model is fit to the whole curve, not this one point: a low backspin lift plus a drag term that grows for steep balls. It tracks the data within a few feet across the range, the steep band included.

The model

Predicting the whole distribution for this exact ball

A gradient-boosted model trained on 1.45 million batted balls predicts a carry distribution for France's exact inputs: 105.3 mph, 49 degrees, down the line, in Milwaukee's still dome air. It is the one method that sees the full conditions. Reading the curve up to 344 feet gives the chance of reaching it. Its 89, 80, and 50 percent intervals cover 89, 79, and 49 percent of held-out balls, so the spread is honest. Conditioned on the dome's dense, still air it sits below the all-conditions rate, because the comparable balls that reached 344 were often helped by warm or thin air the closed roof lacks.

The target

Down the line, the wall sits at 348 feet

Down the left-field line the wall is 348 feet, four deeper than the 344 foot foul pole. And an 8 foot wall is not a line on the ground: a steeply falling ball must land a few feet past its base to clear it. Both make a home run a little harder than just reaching 344.

The air it was hit in

Thin air is worth almost 40 feet of carry

A ball carries farther in thin air. So every one of the 2.5 million balls was joined to the conditions it was hit in: roof and field wind from the MLB game report, hourly humidity and pressure from a weather reanalysis at each park. That gives each ball a true air density and lets the physics be fit across speed, angle, and density together, free of the altitude mix.

Milwaukee's dome is near league-average density, so the pooled estimate holds there, but air is worth a lot. The same ball carries 318 feet in the dome, 305 in cold sea-level air, and 344 at Coors Field's altitude. That Coors carry reaches the 344 foot line, nearly 40 feet more than the densest parks, yet it is still short of the 348 foot wall: even thin air does not quite make it a home run.

Wind we measured rather than assumed. Across 150,000 open-air balls, Statcast's projected carry moves just about 0.2 feet per mph of reported tailwind, far below the roughly 3 feet per mph a ball feeling the full wind would show. The projection is largely wind-neutralized, so wind is not a hidden confound, and the closed roof had none anyway.

The swing

It was a big uppercut, and that put heavy backspin on the ball

Statcast never publishes batted-ball spin, but since 2024 it tracks the bat, and France's swing is on record: a 75.8 mph barrel on a 21.9 degree uppercut against a 98.9 mph fastball. The ball left at 49 degrees, well above the swing path, so the bat undercut it by about 27 degrees. Undercut is what spins a ball, and the collision geometry puts this one near 1,600 to 2,600 rpm: a lot.

That steep launch is inefficient on its own, which is most of why the ball lands short, but the swing did not add to it. Among comparable balls a steeper attack angle like France's carries a touch farther, because reaching 49 degrees with a steeper swing takes less undercut, and so less of the backspin that fights a climbing ball. Fed his real swing instead of a league-typical one, the model raises the estimate slightly. It lands near 320 feet and reaches 344 about 8.5 percent of the time, a hair more than a typical swing.

The answer

Where the three methods land

The three methods rest on different assumptions. They agree on the carry, a central 317 to 322 feet, and the verdict, probably short. On the exact chance of reaching 344 they spread: the two most direct, the empirical pool and the condition-grounded physics, put it at 11 and 12 percent; the machine-learning models, conditioned on the exact dome air, sit lower at 5 to 7 percent, with the bat-tracking model at about 8.5. The raw column reaches the fence on the ground; the wall column also clears 8 feet while staying fair, the real bar for a home run.

Caveats

What we still can't measure

  • Backspin is estimated, not measured. Public Statcast gives no batted-ball spin, so the swing only bounds it (about 1,600 to 2,600 rpm). A collision model built to pin it from the swing barely helped: the swing explains only ~7 percent of the carry scatter, because the spread at a fixed launch is set by the exact contact point, which bat tracking can't see. Hawk-Eye tracked the real flight up to the cable, but those positions and any measured spin stay with MLB, not the public feed. So the answer is a distribution, not a point.
  • Carry is a projection. Statcast hit distance is a modeled flight distance, not a surveyed landing, and carries its own error, though it appears largely wind-neutral.
  • Field wind is still uncertain. The reported wind is a station reading, not the wind inside the bowl; we measured how little the projection responds to it, but the true field wind stays loosely pinned.
  • The park and roof are approximate. Wall distances are interpolated from a few published anchors, and the roof state is best-effort from the game report.

Most likely a long out: about a one-in-eight shot to reach the fence, closer to one in sixteen to clear it for a home run.
